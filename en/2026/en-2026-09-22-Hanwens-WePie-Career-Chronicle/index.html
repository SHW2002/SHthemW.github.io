Hanwen's WePie Career Chronicle

Posted on 2026-09-22 In 游戏开发 Views:

Shen Hanwen (ShenHanWen / SHW2002) · A review of work and growth, 2024–2026

Shen Hanwen’s time at WePie can be traced back to a fishing UI. At first, the questions were fixed headings and scrolling lists. Later, his records included cross-platform migration, animation synchronization, and code generation. Together they show a gradual expansion of responsibility: finish a feature, make it survive the whole workflow, then prepare reusable foundations for the next development effort and the colleagues taking it over.

In 2024, as internship gave way to graduation, he entered commercial game development. Computers and games had long been interests; joining a team gave those interests concrete form: live versions, assets awaiting integration, requirements to confirm together, and player feedback after release. Neverland (梦幻岛), Hide and Seek (躲猫猫), and Sunny Island (《小岛有晴天》) each contributed a different experience. Across them, he learned to solve immediate problems more completely and leave the solutions behind.

1 · 2024–2025 · Neverland: building business foundations in a mature project

Neverland was an important starting point. Its rich gameplay and accumulated rules required a newcomer to learn while working. Hanwen began with UI, interaction, and presentation issues, gradually contributing to fishing progression, tackle, and vehicles as connected feature groups. His annual review singled out Neverland 2.6 going live on WePlay’s Chinese-language service: his first experience contributing to development and following a release all the way through. Players’ enthusiasm gave direct feedback. Completing that first delivery made the lesson concrete at every entry point: after a button press, server data, UI, and character state must all support the result.

1.1 Fishing and vehicles: from a first release to clear state responsibilities

  • Complete a first launch: contribute to fishing development in Neverland 2.6 and its Chinese-language release, building experience from requirements through deployment.

  • Distinguish character states: separate “in a vehicle” from “driving,” establishing boundaries for passenger movement, driving, and leaving a boat.

    • isInVehicle and isDriving represent boarding and driving separately. Driving requires already being aboard; leaving also exits driving.
    • Camera and movement modules subscribe to the relevant state changes, integrating passenger and driver behavior through separate interfaces.

Much of this learning happened in ordinary exchanges. For the fish-basket UI, he checked navigation, fixed regions, scrolling, and sizing with a UI designer, used existing screens to clarify behavior, and confirmed the implemented result, earning the designer’s approval. Similar distinctions in vehicles became state interfaces and module responsibilities. The progress lay in specifics: first understand how one screen should be used, then distinguish how several gameplay systems share one character.

1.2 Refactoring GM tools: extensible commands that are easy to find

As fishing and vehicles expanded, the team’s debug commands grew. Developers and testers needed to locate business operations quickly and keep common ones in fixed places. Hanwen organized these needs into a new GM tool. It brought operations from different features together for daily debugging, reproduction, and verification. Its use spread beyond developers as collaboration broadened.


Neverland · Excerpt from the GM categories and favorites panel

  • Give commands a common home: register categories and commands at runtime, reusing the same panel presentation and execution flow as gameplay expands.

    • Extract command management from NLGMPanel into NLGMTool, representing names, categories, and execution functions as registrable data.
    • Generate lists centrally and record results and errors consistently; business modules add commands in the same way.

  • Keep common operations close at hand: add favorites that survive restarts. Testers confirmed that finding and explaining entry points became easier and suggested similar improvements for Sunny Island.

    • Store favorites using command-name keys, restore them on startup, reject duplicates, and clean persistent storage after removal.
    • Improve handover through development-team trials and tester instructions, bringing favorites and categories into daily debugging.

From command organization and panel integration to persistence, Hanwen delivered a complete tool colleagues could use, then improved it through trials, instructions, and feedback. It was an early lesson in delivery: an improvement he proposed and implemented must also work in somebody else’s day. Shared registration helped developers add commands, while other roles could find the operations they needed. Daily use confirmed the tool’s value.

1.3 The first Unity Autorun: automate repeated game-entry steps

Entering the game meant a familiar sequence: run the editor, wait for scene loading, connect services, and enter gameplay. These stable, repeated steps offered a clear automation target. Autorun began there, letting presets take over fixed operations while developers used loading time for other work. The small tool turned repeated daily actions into a reusable workflow.

  • Automate repetitive clicks: entering Neverland previously involved about 30 seconds of waiting and manual operations; presets took over the clicking portion.

    • Presets record button names or text, UI types, and waits, execute them in sequence, and support exit steps before stopping a run.

  • Grow from one project to three: saved configurations cover three projects—Neverland, the store, and Hide and Seek—with real entry and exit steps.

    • Save settings by project and configure them through a GUI; add tutorials responding to colleagues’ confusion and disable Run for empty presets.

After handover, he actively asked about difficulties and updated the tutorial for configuring a freshly checked-out project and using the GUI. Trial discussions gradually improved configuration, documentation, and guidance, making the tool easier to use in colleagues’ own projects. It later crossed into Sunny Island and evolved into a navigation graph. The early settings, documentation, and feedback began that thread.

1.4 Migrating Neverland fishing and vehicles: toward complete delivery

Moving fishing and vehicles from the Chinese-language service to Huiwan took the work beyond the familiar project. Native capabilities, backend connectivity, and interactions between boarding and Huiwan-specific flight or other gameplay all needed confirmation. Hanwen organized call relationships, test scope, and remaining work, then coordinated priorities, support, and regression with the team. Previously focused on feature implementation, he now saw how a release passed between roles on its way to production.

  • All 83 checks passed: that round of vehicle smoke testing for Neverland 2.7 contained 83 cases, all 83 passing—a 100% pass rate, confirmed by a tester in the project group.

    • Trace Unity calls to native clients and backends, identifying exact methods and endpoints for item previews, friends, rewards, and image loading.
    • Add verification priorities for flight, room changes, boarding, and wedding-island boundaries around vehicle room relationships and local coordinates.

  • One fewer request for reward indicators: the pre-refresh path for album reward badges reduced proactive CollectOverview requests from one per refresh to zero, reusing existing pushes.

    • Confirm album data already arrives by push, then remove the duplicate request before badge evaluation. The business module owns seasonal click records; the general layer owns nodes and visibility conditions.
    • Include refresh after acquisition, redemption, use, and gifting in verification, delivering both optimization and checking requirements.

Migration preparation connected interfaces, gameplay, and verification. Knowledge from the old environment became checks he could explain in the new one. He also actively performed Code Review in related versions, bringing his implementations into shared inspection. Testing, regression, and launch then followed explicit priorities. He progressed from contributing features to organizing delivery of his own area, translating technical judgment into plans the team could execute.

Badge optimization followed the same approach. Clear ownership of business rules and shared capabilities produced clearer interfaces; fewer requests came with explicit verification of refresh behavior. The optimization included both implementation and checks, so collaborators knew what changed and what to inspect. These practices became habits when maintaining existing features.

  • Integrate new vehicles through a shared process: organize asset configuration, type-specific rules, and exit flow separately, supported by an editor and authoring instructions.

    • Configure position, actions, and camera per asset; vehicle types implement rules such as sitting on a jet ski.
    • Use ExitContext and prepareExitVehicle to connect standing, leaving driver mode, and disembarking, documenting and teaching the workflow.

Vehicle expansion extended these lessons into asset production. Editors, documentation, and group explanations handed code conventions to artists, giving model, animation, and camera preparation a common process. The output now included interfaces for subsequent vehicles and authoring instructions users could follow. Development considered both how features run and how other roles continue creating content.

1.5 Log cleanup: finding removable cost in high-frequency output

After business implementation, Hanwen began looking at runtime behavior. Debug logs revealed execution, and organizing them by purpose and frequency became a concrete optimization direction. He sampled from game entry, ranked frequent sources, explained retaining important network/resource information at a development meeting, and pushed prioritized release of the changes. Optimization started by understanding what was happening.

  • Quiet more than nine hundred repeated logs: in a sample from entering Neverland until five seconds after frame-rate stabilization, one message appeared over 900 times. He recorded noticeably improved smoothness after removing it.

    • Locate frequent sources by count, retain key network/resource diagnostics, and prioritize releasing the relevant changes.
    • Add a default-off debug switch to badge tooling and log tags, then continue organizing shared loading, network, and character output.

This extended into routine maintenance: control output according to need while preserving diagnostic switches for investigation. Several Neverland work streams now converged—business implementation, tooling, and runtime observation supported a live system together. Hanwen increasingly looked beyond feature completion to how it ran, leaving reusable maintenance practices for the next development round.

2 · Late 2024 to early 2025 · Hide and Seek: real-time gameplay and multiplayer synchronization

While Neverland continued iterating, Hanwen joined Hide and Seek. Built on the earlier Ao Oni project, it could reuse assets but needed reorganized rules. His focus shifted toward characters: movement, collision, camera, and animation must cooperate in one operation, and other clients must see the result after a local button press. Learning old code and establishing new rules happened together, bringing boundary decisions into the core of real-time gameplay.

2.1 Real-time gameplay: adapt flow and character behavior to new rules


Hide and Seek · Counterattack trajectory development and debugging

  • Adapt the main flow and capture behavior: contribute phase flow, pursuer AI, and counterattack skills, integrating movement detection, pursuit, freezing, and human/AI control changes.

    • AI tracks nearby surviving players, pursues when movement triggers it, and returns to patrol after a timeout; freezing, stun, and human-control states consistently constrain behavior.
    • Retain existing position synchronization while implementing counterattack ascent/descent in model presentation. Validate charging locally, play remotely from release messages, and handle early collision cleanup.

  • A large test with zero bugs: according to the designer’s review, the final large-scale test found zero bugs. Test updates also confirmed version 1.0 testing was complete and quality stable.

These divisions translated rules into character behavior and gave local input, remote presentation, and control handover distinct responsibilities. Development required understanding the existing framework and selecting suitable places for new rules; integration required checking message/state relationships with the server team. Movement, presentation, and input became clearer, letting the existing project support new real-time gameplay.

Hanwen worked with his mentor and another client developer. Designers and testers recognized the stable stage-test results. While still gaining full-project experience, he learned that inherited framework constraints could still support delivery through clear responsibilities. The experience later reappeared in Sunny Island’s separation of local decisions from remote presentation: projects changed, but carefully understood problems remained useful.

2.2 Lua annotations: from cross-language needs to upstream contribution

A tooling thread continued across projects. Neverland and Hide and Seek both used Lua and C#, making reliable cross-language hints an everyday concern. Hanwen first asked his mentor how editors recognize annotations, then modified type generation and organized real needs into an existing open-source generator. He grew from a configuration user into a contributor able to repair and extend the tool.

  • Bring project patches upstream: six categories of capabilities, including generic inference, aliases, and function compatibility, were merged through PR #2. Project adaptations became part of the original tool’s public version.

    • Refine common generic inheritance so members such as Singleton<T>.Instance receive useful hints; express C# delegate/Lua function compatibility through union types.
    • Split annotation files, save generation options, and document language-service directories and compatibility settings for repeated practical use.

Real project calls guided the changes, and usage gradually filled out the instructions. The repository went to colleagues for trials before upstream submission, connecting generation, editor settings, and public maintenance. The merge was a concrete result; the lasting change was that a local solution, once organized, could serve other developers and build his open-source collaboration experience.

3 · Late 2025–2026 · Sunny Island: from character control to synchronization architecture

Sunny Island brought a substantial change in responsibility. His annual review described his first complete ownership of a game module from the ground up: designing, implementing, and iterating 3C within the existing framework, organizing character, camera, and controls into foundations for future gameplay. Requirements sometimes began as reference videos. He analyzed controls, built playable versions to align with designers, then organized synchronization, configuration, and assets. Earlier experience became a sustained area of ownership.

3.1 First full 3C ownership: exploration through continued delivery

  • Own the core module’s whole cycle: from reference analysis and playable validation to implementation and iteration, continue building Sunny Island’s 3C.

    • Make an experiential version first, check controls and presentation with designers, then implement confirmed rules in character and animation systems.
    • Refine interfaces and settings for new content so the module supports later personal actions and complex interactions.

  • Reproduce playback remotely: use StateNameHash, continuous parameters, and NextState for state passthrough, with generated ClipOnly controllers, forming reusable synchronization for subsequent personal actions.

    • Make decisions locally and play remotely from state information; generate ClipOnly controllers from the same source machine while retaining necessary automatic exits.
    • Pre-blend through NextState, collect requests by layer, read states one frame later, and identify replay using playback time.


ClipOnly controller generator · GitHub README excerpt · 2026-09-22

  • Document the technical result: write “Implementing Animation Synchronization with Pass-Through States in Unity Mecanim,” including scope and supporting tools.

    • Explain suitability for small social scenes, the design’s evolution, and companion generators for later reference and reuse.

Animation synchronization became a representative outcome of complete ownership. Later actions reused it; asset changes had generation support; the implementation and boundaries became an article. His own design kept serving new content, strengthening his grasp of the core module: make technical choices, deliver them, and maintain them through real use.

3.2 Export tooling and conventions: encode experience in generation rules

Once he owned a core module, peripheral work deserved earlier organization too. More settings meant accumulating costs from handwritten IDs, copied generation scripts, and collisions. The Neverland habit of tooling repeated operations reached the connection between data and code in Sunny Island. Fixing a table keeps today’s version moving; generation rules also provide a way to integrate the next table.

  • Eight output flows, one maintenance point: consolidate eight repetitive C# output flows into one shared implementation, used by six generator types and then NPCs, covering seven types in total.

    • Extend Luban with ItemId constants, standardizing system/name/ID references; validate enums and append IDs with warnings for duplicate names.
    • Centralize parameter checks, structure, comments, and output in UGFIdGeneratorBase; discover and run generators together while retaining each table’s reading and naming rules.
    • Skip unchanged writes, reuse shared naming steps, and integrate new types such as NPCs through the same flow.

The growth was in turning personal maintenance experience into project rules. Generated names standardized references, generation checked enums and collisions, and common output had one owner. Conventions gained an executable form that later types could reuse. Early tooling habits became engineering foundations: a solution from one task became a condition already available for the team’s next task.

Personal-action delivery tested both architecture and working methods. After playback, synchronization, and configuration, Hanwen ran smoke cases and documented completion, asset arrangements, verification, and later integration points. Module ownership covered implementation, self-testing, integration, and testing handover. Leads and testers could plan from these records, while designers could continue adding content through existing settings.

These responses marked a shift from assigned-task completion to responsibility for delivery. Stage self-tests, clear asset handover, and follow-up gave his modules complete closure; low stage defect counts and tester approval provided practical feedback. Animation tools and authoring instructions continued serving later versions. Independent ownership grew through implementation, verification, and handover.

3.4 Authoring conventions: deliver technical systems as team workflows

From Neverland’s vehicle editor to Sunny Island’s Timeline, Hanwen increasingly considered implementation and user handover together. He saved Timeline binding data and restored runtime references, then documented authoring, saving, reloading, and acceptance. Code supplied capability; documentation explained order and responsibilities so designers, artists, and programmers could proceed. Technical experience became a repeatable team process.

  • Deliver bindings and authoring instructions: own track-target persistence, runtime restoration, and supporting documentation, connecting asset creation with code integration.

    • Save target hierarchy paths and object/component types, restore references at runtime, and reuse a scene object index.
    • Distinguish scene references from prefab-internal objects; explain when to save bindings and how to resave and reload after changes.

  • Include acceptance in authoring: put operations, handover, and expected runtime results in one usable reference.

    • Explain requirements such as restoring camera control afterward, so creators know what to check beyond the visible effect.
    • Document held, preview, and placed entity responsibilities, configuration relationships, and minimum acceptance checks.

His responsibility had expanded. Earlier he asked colleagues to clarify UI and asset rules; now he explained tools and delivery himself. Vehicle documentation and group teaching grew into more complete operation and acceptance instructions. His output included steps other roles could follow and references for handling specific situations, beyond code alone.

3.5 Collection displays and newcomer collaboration: supporting shared delivery

The collection feature displayed existing characters and crops in a new way. Combining characters with enlarged crops made size, pose, and loading order significant. Unexpectedly tiny characters led investigation into skinning and bones. Reusing the team’s scene entities, Hanwen handled concentrated technical display work while a newcomer developed the main collection feature. He was now finishing key modules while supporting someone else’s development.

    • Wait for clothing, items, skinning updates, and a stable pose before measuring current world bounds.
    • Exclude hidden parts and particles; scale uniformly using the most restrictive axis for characters combined with oversized crops.

  • Extend beyond one display: colleagues extended the collection-leaderboard animation driver into two later areas—wish-privilege previews and team-event rankings.

    • AnimDriverScenePlayer reuses clip loading, caching, and overrides while separating display poses from real player control.
    • Separate “retrieve actual clothing” from “load a model by slot,” enabling wish try-ons and other display features.

  • Share development experience with a newcomer: alongside his display module, participate in collection code review, protocol guidance, and test preparation.

    • Explain using generated PB structures and compiling after generation before committing, establishing consistent data integration.
    • Review collection detail pages, share findings, and guide UI state organization and early communication about dependencies.
    • Confirm configuration, self-test, and integration progress; include review and smoke verification in test preparation.

His responsibility now included shared delivery. The newcomer drove main functionality while Hanwen handled 3D presentation, requirements checks, support, and review; the lead asked him to keep following the newcomer’s work. From asking a mentor to explaining interfaces and integration to a newcomer, the role changed through concrete collaboration. He began explaining his judgment and bringing code checks, configuration, and handover into shared progress.

3.7 Complex movement and two-player interaction: transport, pose, and timing

Rope movement, hand-holding, and intimate actions made the character system coordinate more at once. One person’s turn must be followed by the other’s position, hands, and animation, while observers receive data on their own schedule. Animation drivers, state boundaries, resource lifecycles, and networking converged in one experience. Hanwen separated stutter, misalignment, and joint twitching, located their causes, then made the fixes cooperate toward continuous motion.

  • Slow rope snapshot transmission: the minimum interval increased from 50 ms to 100 ms, changing nominal frequency from 20 Hz to 10 Hz—a 50% reduction. Receivers retained interpolation and short extrapolation.

    • Return special-gameplay scheduling to the shared character interval, leaving continuous presentation to receivers and maintaining transport separately from visual processing.


Sunny Island · Two-player interaction development

Rope tradeoffs extended into two-person interaction. Hand-holding needs stable shared position and pose while leaving room to tune different actions. Hanwen separated shared contact, turning follow, and joint pose, established their calculation and update order, then integrated them into one flow. Experience adjustments now corresponded to specific mechanisms, and new resources could follow existing rules.

  • Three issues resolved the same day: his update to the lead reported three bugs found in that test round after the initial experience, with those and smoke-test findings fixed that day.

  • Start and end paired actions together: each client prepares both players’ resources, then applies both positions and actions on the same local frame, handling messages and cleanup by relationship ID, sequence, and phase.

    • Separate resource waiting from playback timing; check relationship IDs, sequence, and phase to reject stale data.
    • Restore positions, remove relationships, and clean Timeline through common phases for entry and exit.

  • Adapt contact and joints together: the initiator controls following and IK targets, with independent shoulder, elbow, and wrist constraints and smoothing.

    • Retain a reference pose for the interaction, smooth a shared contact point in initiator-local space, and apply reachability constraints and asset offsets.
    • Tune turn lag, angular speed, and follow speed separately while preserving ordinary movement rhythm.
    • Reference upper arms to the character and elbows/wrists to parent bones; constrain yaw, pitch, and roll separately and cache final constrained rotations as the next smoothing baseline.

Experience kept shaping the implementation after initial delivery. Preparation, execution, and cleanup gained common phases, while constraints and smoothing became clearer through iteration. Communication and closed requirements recorded the results. Reachable contact, reasonable joints, and complete exit now had distinct mechanisms, giving collaborators boundaries for confirming requirements and tuning the interaction.

  • Smooth locally, transmit less often: periodic paired-pose transmission changed from 30 ms to 100 ms, nominally about 33.3 Hz to 10 Hz—a 70% reduction. Hand IK changed from 30 ms to 200 ms, reaching 5 Hz—an 85% reduction.

    • Place both poses in one sampled snapshot; recipients and observers interpolate along the same timeline. Brief gaps allow bounded extrapolation; teleportation or long gaps rebuild the baseline.
    • Let the initiator calculate and smooth animation speed every frame, with different start/stop thresholds and independent pose, animation, and IK intervals.
    • Self-tests against a non-local server recorded smoother motion, and the straight-line stutter item was subsequently closed.

The results have support in experience feedback, implementation, and transmission settings. Positions, joints, and animation parameters are smoothed separately; network cadence is independently adjustable. Together they create continuous paired presentation. His approach grew from individual fixes into organizing calculation, transport, and playback as a whole. Earlier character-system experience formed a more complete cooperation within this complex interaction.

Afterword: make one delivery the starting point for the next

The growth follows a clear path. Neverland taught commercial development and collaboration through fishing, vehicles, and complete tool delivery. Hide and Seek focused his attention on real-time gameplay and multiplayer synchronization. In Sunny Island, these experiences joined a 3C module under sustained ownership. From understanding systems to choosing designs, implementing, self-testing, and iterating, he learned to own a module completely and leave reusable foundations for later content.

Quality and collaboration gave technical progress concrete responses: stable stage tests, colleague approval, reuse in later business features, and upstream acceptance of open-source improvements. He also documented authoring, encoded naming and validation rules, reviewed code, and supported newcomers. More people could understand and use his methods, extending personal responsibility into shared delivery.

This review stops here for now; exploration continues. The past few years now form experience he can draw on: clarify rules, build the design, verify results, and hand the method to its next user. A finished feature leaves code. Careful delivery leaves quality and trust; organizing those experiences gives the next task a better starting point. That is what “building the world a little at a time” means in this part of his career.
